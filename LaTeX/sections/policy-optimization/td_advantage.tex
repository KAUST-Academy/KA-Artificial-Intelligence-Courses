\begin{frame}{The TD Residual as Advantage}
\begin{itemize}
    \item We introduced $\delta_t$ as the critic's \emph{training} signal. Here is the second, less obvious role it plays --- the one that makes actor-critic work:
    $$\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t) \qquad \text{(the \textbf{TD residual})}$$
    \item When $V_\phi = V^{\pi}$ (a perfect critic), the \textbf{expected} TD residual equals the advantage:
    \begin{align*}
        \mathbb{E}\!\left[\delta_t \mid s_t, a_t\right]
        &= r(s_t,a_t) + \gamma \mathbb{E}_{s_{t+1}}\!\left[V^{\pi}(s_{t+1})\right] - V^{\pi}(s_t) \\
        &= Q^{\pi}(s_t,a_t) - V^{\pi}(s_t) \;=\; A^{\pi}(s_t,a_t)
    \end{align*}
    where $r(s_t,a_t) = \mathbb{E}[r_t \mid s_t, a_t]$ is the expected reward function. In practice we use the observed $r_t$ and a learned $V_\phi$, so $\delta_t$ is an approximation of $A^{\pi}$.
    \pause
    \item So we can use $\delta_t$ as a \textbf{low-variance, online} estimate of $A^{\pi}$ --- no full trajectory needed
    \item This is what Day 3 deferred: we now have a way to estimate $A^{\pi}$ at every timestep
    \vspace{0.5em}
    \item \textbf{One number, two jobs:} $\delta_t$ trains the critic (via $\mathcal{L}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_t[\delta_t^2]$) \emph{and} weights the actor's gradient. That is the whole actor-critic loop.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
